Our Story

Why Unveiled Exists

By Toyosi Osinaike

For a long time, I carried struggles that nobody could see. From the outside, I probably looked fine, but underneath that mask were worries, fears, and challenges I didn't know how to explain.

June 2026

From the outside, I probably looked fine. I smiled when I needed to, said I was okay when people asked, and learned very quickly how to hide the parts of me that were hurting. Like so many young people, I became good at wearing a mask.

But underneath that mask were worries, fears, confusion, and challenges I didn't know how to explain, and even if I had found the words, I'm not sure I believed it was safe to say them out loud.

The truth is, the world didn't always feel like a safe place to open up.

Sometimes it was fear of being misunderstood. Sometimes it was fear of being judged. Sometimes it was fear of being a burden. And sometimes, I simply didn't think anyone would truly understand.

So I stayed quiet.

Looking back now, I can see how much support I might have benefited from if I'd felt safe enough to talk. If I'd had a space where honesty was welcomed instead of feared. If there had been people around me who knew how to ask the deeper questions, not just "Are you okay?" but "How are you, really?"

I know now that silence can become its own kind of struggle.

When young people don't feel safe enough to share what's happening inside, those hidden challenges don't disappear, they often grow heavier. And parents, carers, and families are often left trying their best to help, while only seeing part of the picture.

That gap, between what is felt and what is spoken, is where so much pain lives.

And that is why Unveiled exists.

Unveiled was born from the belief that hidden struggles deserve light.

It exists because too many young people are suffering quietly. Too many parents are trying to support their children without fully understanding what they're carrying. Too many families are navigating emotional challenges feeling isolated, confused, or overwhelmed.

This community exists to change that.

To create a space where masks can come off.

Where hard conversations are met with compassion.

Where young people feel seen, heard, and believed.

Where parents and carers feel supported instead of judged.

Where honesty becomes strength, not something to hide.

I cannot change what my younger self went through.

But I can help build the kind of community I wish had existed back then.

A place where people feel safe enough to say, "I'm struggling."

A place where someone responds, "You don't have to carry this alone."

That is the heart of Unveiled.

Because when we bring hidden struggles into the light, healing becomes possible.

And when we choose openness, compassion, and connection, together, we become the light.

Parents A-Z Guide

A guide for every letter, for every worry.

Inspired by the structure of established children's mental health guidance, written in our own words. Click a letter to browse topics, or read straight through.

When Home Doesn't Feel Safe

Topic: Abuse & Domestic Violence

If you're worried your child has witnessed or experienced abuse, here's what to look for and how to respond.

Abuse can be physical, emotional, sexual, or neglect, and includes children witnessing violence between adults at home, even when it isn't directed at them. It's never the child's fault, and it can affect their mental health both at the time and later in life.

If your child tells you something has happened, stay calm, believe them, and let them know it's not their fault. You don't need all the answers straight away, taking the first step to get professional advice is what matters.

Where to get help: If anyone is in immediate danger, call 999. NSPCC (0808 800 5000) supports any adult worried about a child. Refuge (0808 2000 247) and Women's Aid support those experiencing domestic abuse.

Making Sense of ADHD

Topic: ADHD

Understanding attention, focus, and hyperactivity differences, and how to get an assessment.

ADHD (Attention Deficit Hyperactivity Disorder) affects how a young person's brain manages attention, impulse control, and activity levels. It's a recognised neurodevelopmental condition, not a discipline problem.

A formal diagnosis usually starts with your GP, who can refer your child for an assessment. In the meantime, consistent routines, clear short instructions, and celebrating effort over outcome can make a real difference at home.

Where to get help: Speak to your GP for a referral, or contact CAMHS. YoungMinds and the ADHD Foundation have detailed guidance for families.

When Anger Takes Over

Topic: Anger

Why anger shows up so big in young people, and how to respond without escalating it.

Anger in children and teens is often a cover for something underneath, fear, sadness, embarrassment, or feeling unheard. It's a normal emotion, but it can become a problem when it's frequent, intense, or hard to manage safely.

Try to stay calm yourself, name the feeling ("it sounds like you're really frustrated") rather than the behaviour, and talk about what happened once everyone's calmer, not in the heat of the moment.

Where to get help: If anger is affecting daily life, your GP or school can refer you to local family support services.

Living with Anxiety

Topic: Anxiety

What anxiety looks like in young people, and practical ways to help, both in a panicky moment and over the longer term.

Anxiety is a mix of worried thoughts, uneasy feelings, and physical sensations in the body. Feeling worried sometimes is a normal response to stress, most young people feel it before something like an exam and settle again once it's passed. It becomes a problem when a young person feels stuck in it, and it starts limiting what they feel able to do day to day.

What can make a young person anxious

  • Finding school difficult, or school-specific anxiety
  • Being around someone else who's very anxious, like a parent
  • Big changes, such as moving house, a change of school, or parents separating
  • Bereavement, or the illness of someone close to them
  • Carrying a lot of responsibility, like caring for a family member
  • Family stress around housing, money, or debt
  • Feeling unsafe, such as bullying, abuse, or discrimination
  • Unmet needs around a neurodivergent condition like autism or ADHD

Signs to look out for

  • Physical: feeling sick, tense or fidgety, short of breath, shaky, or struggling to sleep
  • Thoughts: dwelling on scary or negative thoughts, worrying about coping with everyday things
  • Feelings: on edge, panicky, overwhelmed, or a sense of dread
  • Behaviour: withdrawing, avoiding school or social situations, irritability, repeating rituals, or changes in eating

Helping in a panicky moment

The aim here is simply to help them feel safe and calmer, not to solve anything. Try slow breathing together, a grounding exercise (naming things they can see, hear, and touch), sitting close and offering calm reassurance, or gently reminding them that the feeling will pass.

Helping them manage anxiety over time

Away from the panicky moments, it helps to talk about their anxiety when things are calm, without rushing to fix it, and to think together about what's behind it. Building in regular exercise, sleep, and time with people they trust, alongside activities that help them relax, can make a steady difference over time.

Finding professional help

If anxiety is significantly affecting daily life, your GP can talk it through and refer to CAMHS if needed, where support might include CBT (Cognitive Behavioural Therapy) or, in some cases, medication alongside talking therapy. Your child's school can often provide pastoral support alongside any clinical help too.

Where to get help: Speak to your GP as a first step. YoungMinds' Parents Helpline (0808 802 5544) offers advice and emotional support. Anxiety UK (03444 775 774) and No Panic (0300 772 9844) specialise in supporting people with anxiety and panic attacks.

Autism and How It Shapes Wellbeing

Topic: Autism

How autism can affect a young person's mental health, and how to support them well.

Autistic young people experience the world differently, and can be at higher risk of anxiety, low mood, or burnout, especially when masking their autistic traits to fit in.

Understanding your child's specific sensory and communication needs, allowing recovery time after demanding situations, and working with, not against, their way of processing the world.

Where to get help: The National Autistic Society offers guidance and a helpline. Your GP can refer for an autism assessment if needed.

Bullying: Spotting It, Stopping It

Topic: Bullying

Spotting the signs of bullying, how to talk to your child about it, and practical steps to help it stop.

Bullying is any repeated behaviour intended to hurt someone, physically, emotionally, or both. It can happen anywhere, at school, at home, online, or in other social spaces, and it's never okay or something a young person should have to just put up with. Bullying based on someone's race, sexuality, gender identity, religion, disability, or another perceived difference is treated as a hate crime in law.

Signs to look out for

  • Seeming anxious, low, or less confident than usual
  • Avoiding school or a particular place, or often saying they feel unwell
  • Unexplained injuries, or belongings that go missing or get damaged
  • Withdrawing from people, or a drop in schoolwork
  • Sudden changes in how they dress or act, seemingly to "fit in"
  • Eating or sleeping noticeably more or less than usual

There can be other explanations for any of these on their own, so use them as a prompt to check in gently, not as proof on their own.

How to talk to them about it

Bring it up during a shared activity, a walk, a drive, cooking together, rather than as a sit-down "big talk," it can make it easier for them to open up. Start with an "I" statement ("I've noticed you seem really down lately, and I'm wondering if something's going on") rather than a direct question. Listen without jumping in, make clear it's not their fault, and let them know you're there to help, not just to fix it immediately. If they're not ready to talk, don't push, try again another day, or suggest texting or writing it down instead.

How to help stop it

Keep a record from the start, what was said or done, when, and any evidence like photos or screenshots. What happens next depends on where it's happening:

  • At school: read the school's anti-bullying policy, request a meeting to raise it formally, and follow up in writing. If nothing changes, escalate to a more senior staff member, then governors if needed.
  • Online: screenshot everything, report it to the platform, block where possible, and loop in the school if the people involved are from there.
  • Outside school: raise it with whoever's in charge of that space, and involve the school and police if it continues or if anyone's safety is at risk.

If your child, or anyone, is in immediate danger, call 999. Non-emergency reports can go to the police on 101.

Supporting them alongside this

Keep checking in and reassuring them they're loved and don't have to put up with it. Spend time doing things they enjoy together, it helps rebuild confidence and gives them something outside the situation. Encourage time with people who treat them well, and make space for proper rest and switching off, whether that's music, exercise, or something creative.

Where to get help: Speak to your GP if it's affecting their mental health, they can refer for further support. Kidscape (0300 102 4481) specialises in bullying support for parents. NSPCC (0808 800 5000) can advise if you're worried about a child's safety. Childline (0800 1111) offers confidential support directly to your child.

A Parent's Guide to CAMHS

Topic: CAMHS

What CAMHS is, how referrals work, and what to expect from the process.

CAMHS (Child and Adolescent Mental Health Services) is the NHS service for children and young people with mental health difficulties. Referrals usually come through a GP, school, or sometimes self-referral depending on your area.

Ask your GP what the local referral process and waiting times look like. While waiting, early-help services or your child's school pastoral team can often provide interim support.

Where to get help: Start with your GP. YoungMinds has a detailed guide to navigating CAMHS.

What's Behind Challenging Behaviour

Topic: Challenging Behaviour

Understanding what's behind difficult behaviour, and how to respond constructively.

Challenging behaviour is often communication, a young person struggling to express a need, feeling, or difficulty in words, especially under stress.

Try to notice patterns (when, where, what happens before), stay calm and consistent with boundaries, and address the underlying feeling rather than only the behaviour itself.

Where to get help: If it's affecting daily life, your GP, school, or a family support service can help identify what's going on underneath.

Finding the Right Kind of Therapy

Topic: Counselling & Therapy

What different types of therapy involve, and how to find the right support.

Therapy can mean different things, from school counselling to CBT (Cognitive Behavioural Therapy) to specialist NHS or private services. The right type depends on what your child is going through.

Ask your GP, school, or CAMHS what's available locally and whether there's a waiting list. Some charities offer free or low-cost counselling for young people.

Where to get help: Place2Be provides school-based counselling. Your GP can advise on NHS and low-cost options.

Depression in Young People

Topic: Depression & Low Mood

Recognising when low mood becomes something more, and how to support your child.

Everyone has low days, but depression is a persistent low mood and loss of interest in things they used to enjoy, and can affect sleep, appetite, and energy for weeks or longer.

Keep communication open without pressuring them to "snap out of it," encourage small routines (getting outside, regular meals, sleep), and take it seriously enough to seek professional advice.

Where to get help: Speak to your GP. Samaritans (116 123) offer free 24/7 emotional support for anyone struggling.

Helping Your Child Through Separation

Topic: Divorce & Separation

Helping your child cope with family separation in an age-appropriate way.

Family separation is a major life change for a child, and how they're supported through it matters more than the separation itself for their long-term wellbeing.

Keep conflict away from your child where possible, reassure them it's not their fault, and try to keep routines (school, friendships, activities) as stable as you can.

Where to get help: Family Lives (0808 800 2222) offers free advice for parents navigating separation.

Talking to Your Child About Drugs and Alcohol

Topic: Drugs & Alcohol

How to talk to your child about drugs and alcohol, spot when it's becoming a problem, and where to get specialist support.

It's normal for teenagers to experiment, test boundaries, and take some risks, drinking alcohol is common among teenagers, and a smaller number will try illegal drugs or vaping. Reasons vary: curiosity, fitting in, confidence, or as a way of coping with difficult feelings. Talking about it openly, rather than avoiding the subject, helps your child understand the risks and make safer choices.

The law and the risks

Alcohol and nicotine are legal from 18; drugs like cannabis and cocaine are illegal at any age. Whatever the substance, using it affects judgement and decision-making, which raises the risk of dangerous situations, a bad reaction, or simply taking too much.

How it can affect mental health

In the short term, mood can swing while using and afterwards, feeling flat, anxious, or on edge. Over the longer term, misuse can contribute to more serious mental health difficulties, and because teenage brains are still developing, young people can be more vulnerable to this than adults. It can also make existing difficulties like anxiety or depression harder to manage, especially if drugs or alcohol are being used as a way to cope.

How to talk to them about it

Make it an ongoing, everyday conversation rather than a single "big talk," and answer their questions honestly, it's fine to say you'll find something out if you don't know. Stay calm even if what they tell you is worrying, reacting with anger or shouting tends to make young people more secretive, and you can't help keep them safe if you don't know what's going on. Be clear about the risks without being judgemental, and agree how they'll stay safe when they're out, including that they can always call you if a situation feels unsafe.

Peer pressure

If they feel pressured to drink or use drugs, remind them that the decision is always theirs, and that a real friend wouldn't push them to do something they're uncomfortable with. Pressuring someone often says more about the person doing the pressuring than about the person being pressured, that framing can help take some of the sting out of it.

When to be more concerned

Occasional experimentation is common, but it's not normal for a young person to drink or use drugs alone, do so frequently, or feel they "need" it to cope with social situations. Other signs of a bigger problem include noticeable mood changes, losing interest in things they used to enjoy, becoming secretive, money or alcohol going missing, or needing more each time to feel the same effect.

Getting help

Start with your GP, they can talk it through and refer to your local drug and alcohol service, which for under-18s is often through CAMHS. If your child needs urgent medical help after drinking or taking something, call 999 straightaway.

Where to get help: FRANK (0300 123 6600) offers confidential, non-judgemental drug and alcohol advice, for young people and parents. We Are With You offers free support if you're worried about someone else's drinking or drug use. If you need support for yourself while going through this with your child, Samaritans (116 123) and Al-Anon Family Groups (0800 0086 811) are both there for that too.

Getting Support Before Things Escalate

Topic: Early Help

What early help is, when it applies, and how referral and support actually works.

Early help, sometimes called early intervention, is local authority support offered when a difficulty first develops, or doesn't yet meet the threshold for specialist services like social services or CAMHS. The aim is to build strategies and strengths before problems escalate, and it often involves a team of professionals, family support workers, mental health nurses, health visitors, or therapists, depending on what's needed. It's usually voluntary and can only go ahead with your consent.

What situations it can help with

  • Disabilities, special educational needs, or long-term health issues
  • Mild or emerging mental health difficulties
  • Bullying, whether experienced or displayed
  • Difficult relationships at home, including domestic abuse, or a parent or sibling's own mental or physical health
  • Being a young carer or a young parent
  • Being in care, or returning home after being in care
  • Financial or housing difficulties

What support can look like

It varies widely, help accessing school or health services, parenting programmes, activity or mentoring groups, speech and language or emotional support, relationship support, or help improving housing. It can happen at home, at school, at a health centre, or elsewhere, individually or in a group.

How to access it

The first step is usually an Early Help Assessment, which can be suggested by a teacher, SENCO, health visitor, or GP, or in most areas you can self-refer directly to your local authority. The assessment covers your family's situation, any existing support, the challenges you're facing, and what you feel would help, your child may be involved too, depending on their age. Provision and waiting lists vary a lot by area, search your local authority's website with the term "early help" or "Local Offer" to find what's available where you live.

What happens after assessment

If early help can support your family, a family support worker draws up an early help plan, setting out objectives, the support to be provided, and a named Lead Professional coordinating it. Progress gets reviewed regularly, sometimes through a Team Around the Child or Team Around the Family meeting, and support can be extended, ended, or stepped up to services like CAMHS or social services depending on how things go.

Where to get help: Ask your GP, your child's school, or search your local council's website for "early help." Family Lives (0808 800 2222) and Family Line (0808 802 6666) can also help you navigate what's available while you wait.

Spotting the Early Signs of an Eating Disorder

Topic: Eating Disorders

Recognising early warning signs, and how to approach a sensitive conversation.

Eating disorders are serious mental health conditions that can affect anyone, involving a difficult relationship with food, eating, or body image. They're not a lifestyle choice or about vanity.

If you're worried, raise it gently and without judgement, focus on how they're feeling rather than what or how much they're eating, and seek professional support early, early intervention makes a real difference.

Where to get help: Speak to your GP as a first step. The National Alliance for Eating Disorders offers specialist helpline support.

Surviving Exam Season

Topic: Exam Stress

Supporting your child through exam pressure without adding to the stress.

Exam periods can bring intense pressure, affecting sleep, mood, and anxiety levels, especially for young people who already struggle with perfectionism or worry.

Encourage breaks, sleep, and food as much as revision, keep perspective on what exams do and don't determine, and let them know your support isn't conditional on results.

Where to get help: Childline has resources specifically on managing exam stress.

When Gaming Becomes a Worry

Topic: Gaming

Understanding healthy versus harmful gaming habits, and how to set boundaries.

Gaming is a normal part of many young people's lives and can offer real social connection, but it can also become a way of avoiding difficult feelings or escalate into a harder-to-manage habit.

Focus on the function gaming serves for your child (connection, escape, achievement) rather than just the hours, and agree boundaries together rather than imposing them unilaterally where possible.

Where to get help: If gaming is significantly affecting daily life, your GP can advise on further support.

Navigating Mental Health Services

Topic: Mental Health Services

Where to start if you think your child needs professional mental health support.

Navigating mental health services for the first time can feel overwhelming, there are NHS, school, charity, and private routes, and it's not always clear where to begin.

Start with your GP or your child's school pastoral lead, both can refer into local services and explain what's realistically available and how long waits might be.

Where to get help: YoungMinds' Parents Helpline (0808 802 5544) can talk you through your options.

Making Your GP Appointment Count

Topic: GP Support

How to make the most of a GP appointment about your child's mental health.

GPs are usually the first step into further mental health support for a young person, whether that's a referral, a review, or reassurance.

Before the appointment, jot down what you've noticed, when it started, and any changes in behaviour, sleep, or mood, it helps the GP understand the full picture in a short appointment.

Where to get help: Book a routine GP appointment and mention it's about your child's emotional wellbeing when booking.

Supporting a Grieving Child

Topic: Grief & Loss

Supporting a child through bereavement or loss, how to talk about it, and where grief-specific support can help.

Grief is the natural response to losing someone or something important, and it affects everyone differently, there's no set timeline for it. Children and young people can move in and out of grief rather than through it in tidy stages, sometimes seeming fine at school or with friends while still struggling underneath. Feelings can resurface unexpectedly, around anniversaries, birthdays, or for no clear reason at all, and that's normal, not a sign something's going wrong.

How grief can show up

Emotionally, it can look like sadness, anger, anxiety, numbness, guilt, or loneliness, sometimes all in the same day. Physically, it can show up as unexpected waves of crying, headaches, stomach aches, changes in eating or sleep, exhaustion, or trouble concentrating. If you've been affected by the same loss, you may recognise some of this in yourself too, and it's worth looking after your own wellbeing alongside theirs.

Talking to them about it

Explain what's happened clearly and honestly, in an age-appropriate way, children can often sense when something serious has happened, and vague explanations tend to leave them more frightened, not less. Give them space to ask questions, even the same one repeatedly, and focus on listening rather than trying to fix or reassure too quickly. If they seem to feel guilty or responsible in any way, be clear that what happened was not their fault. If they don't want to talk directly, try alongside an activity instead, walking, cooking, or just sitting together without pressure to speak.

Helping them feel safe and supported

Give them different ways to express what they're feeling beyond conversation, writing, drawing, music, or a memory box of photos and keepsakes can all help. Let them know it's okay to keep remembering and talking about the person they've lost, and okay to still enjoy things and laugh without it meaning they've "moved on" too fast. If it feels right for your family, involving them in funerals or memorials, and preparing them for what to expect, can help them make sense of what's happened. It's also worth looping in their school, grief can affect concentration, attendance, and confidence, and a heads-up to a trusted staff member can go a long way.

Sudden or traumatic loss

When a death is sudden or traumatic, shock, numbness, and disbelief are common in the days and weeks after, more than anything else, your child needs reassurance and stability. If they seem persistently overwhelmed, unable to function day to day, or are experiencing flashbacks or ongoing distress, it's worth seeking extra support from your GP.

Where to get help: Winston's Wish (08088 020 021) offers practical bereavement support for children and families. Child Bereavement UK (0800 02 888 40) and Grief Encounter (0808 802 0111) both offer specialist grief support for young people. Your GP can help if grief is significantly affecting daily life or mental health.

If Your Child Needs Hospital-Based Care

Topic: Inpatient Care

What it means if your child needs hospital-based mental health treatment.

Inpatient care is used when a young person's mental health needs more intensive, round-the-clock support than can be provided at home, it's relatively rare and used when other support hasn't been enough.

Ask the clinical team plenty of questions, stay involved in your child's care plan, and look after your own wellbeing too, this is a difficult time for the whole family.

Where to get help: Your CAMHS team will guide you through this process if it becomes relevant.

Staying Calm as a Family

Topic: Mindful Parenting

Simple, everyday ways to help your child settle during big emotions, and stay steadier yourself while you do it.

Mindful parenting means noticing what's happening, in your child and in yourself, before reacting, rather than responding on autopilot when things feel stressful. It isn't about being calm all the time, that's not realistic for anyone, it's about building small pauses and habits that make a thoughtful response more likely, more often.

Calming techniques to try together

  • Slow breathing together, in for a count, out for a slightly longer count, helps both of you settle physically before talking anything through
  • Naming what you can see, hear, and touch around you can interrupt a spiral of big feelings and bring you both back to the present moment
  • A consistent, calm bedtime routine, same order, same time, screens off beforehand, helps the whole household wind down and sleep better
  • Short, regular check-ins, even something as simple as "how are you feeling today, out of ten?", build the habit of noticing feelings before they become overwhelming
  • Modelling your own calm, even narrating it out loud ("I'm feeling frustrated, so I'm going to take a breath first"), teaches them that big feelings are manageable, not something to fear

Building it into family life

These habits work best as something woven into everyday life, not just pulled out in a crisis. A few minutes of calm, screen-free time together each day, and a predictable bedtime wind-down, tend to do more over time than any single technique used only when things have already gone wrong.

Where to get help: If your child's big feelings are frequent, intense, or hard to manage safely, that's worth raising with your GP or school rather than relying on these techniques alone. Apps and guided content aimed at families, such as Headspace's family content, can be a useful supplement alongside professional support if things escalate.

When Money Stress Reaches Your Child

Topic: Money & Mental Health

How financial pressure at home can affect young people, and what can help.

Children are often more aware of family financial stress than parents realise, and money worries at home can contribute to anxiety, low mood, or a sense of responsibility that isn't theirs to carry.

Age-appropriate honesty, without burdening them with adult decisions, and reassurance that financial stress is a family issue to solve together, not theirs to fix.

Where to get help: Family Lives (0808 800 2222) can offer support and signposting for family financial stress.

What OCD Really Looks Like

Topic: OCD

Recognising OCD beyond the stereotypes, and how to respond supportively.

OCD involves unwanted, intrusive thoughts (obsessions) and repetitive behaviours or mental rituals (compulsions) done to reduce anxiety, it's often misunderstood and more distressing than people assume.

Avoid reassuring compulsions too readily as it can reinforce the cycle, stay calm and patient, and support your child in getting a proper assessment rather than trying to manage it alone.

Where to get help: OCD Action offers a helpline and specialist information. Your GP can refer for assessment.

Parenting Through Your Own Struggles

Topic: Parenting & Mental Health

Looking after your own mental health while supporting your child's.

Parenting with your own mental health condition is common and doesn't make you a bad parent, but it can be hard to know how much to share with your child or how to look after yourself alongside them.

Be honest in an age-appropriate way if it feels right, build your own support network, and remember that looking after your own wellbeing is part of looking after theirs.

Where to get help: Family Lives and your GP can point you toward local parent support groups.

How Racism Affects a Young Person's Mind

Topic: Racism & Mental Health

Understanding the impact of racism on a young person's mental health, and how to support them through it.

Racism can happen anywhere, at school, at work, at home, online, or even within families and relationships. Sometimes it's obvious, verbal abuse, stereotyping, or violence, and sometimes it's part of wider systems and structures, or subtle enough that other people struggle to see it. Your child may also feel affected by racism that happens to people like them, even when it isn't aimed at them directly, and that's a real and valid response, not an overreaction.

What matters most is how the situation makes your child feel, not whether other people agree it "counts." Being doubted or dismissed after sharing an experience like this can feel very isolating, so believing them and taking it seriously is one of the most protective things you can do.

How to support them

Have open, ongoing conversations about race and racism rather than treating it as a one-off, difficult subject, this helps your child feel it's genuinely safe to bring things to you as they come up. Keep language age-appropriate: simpler, values-based conversations about fairness and respect for younger children, and a more led-by-them, confidential space for older children to share their own experiences. Encourage them to stay connected to their identity and community, and to find people, groups, or movements where they feel understood.

It's also worth thinking together about what they're seeing online, since exposure to distressing content or discourse can affect mood significantly. For younger children, age-appropriate news sources and conversation can help; for older children, encourage them to curate their feed, mute or block what upsets them, and report abuse where it happens.

Signs it may be affecting their mental health

  • Persistent low mood, low self-esteem, or feeling numb
  • Noticeable changes in sleep or eating habits
  • Ongoing anxiety or worry
  • A negative shift in how they see their own appearance or ethnic identity
  • Flashbacks or intrusive thoughts about a specific incident

Getting professional help

Your GP is usually the first step, and they can refer to further support including CAMHS if needed. You're also entitled to ask to see a GP from a similar background if that would make the conversation easier, you don't have to justify the request, it's a reasonable thing to ask for. If you ever feel a mental health professional is treating you or your child unfairly because of race, you have the right to raise a formal complaint through the service's own complaints procedure.

Where to get help: Black Minds Matter connects Black individuals and families with free professional mental health support. BAATN (The Black, African and Asian Therapy Network) lists therapists with specific expertise in this area. Muslim Youth Helpline (0808 808 2008) offers culturally sensitive support for young Muslims. Stop Hate UK's 24/7 helpline supports anyone experiencing hate crime or discrimination.

When Your Child Won't Go to School

Topic: School Anxiety

Understanding why a child might struggle to attend school, and how to help.

School anxiety and refusal is usually a sign of genuine distress, not defiance, it can stem from bullying, academic pressure, social difficulties, or anxiety unrelated to school itself.

Try to understand what specifically feels hard rather than focusing only on attendance, and work with the school's pastoral team on a gradual, supported return rather than an all-or-nothing approach.

Where to get help: Your child's school SENCO or pastoral lead is a good first point of contact, alongside your GP.

Building a Steadier Sense of Self

Topic: Self-Esteem

What shapes a young person's self-esteem, the signs it's struggling, and practical ways to help rebuild it.

Self-esteem is how we think and feel about ourselves, our sense that we're worthy of good things, that our strengths are real, and that it's okay to be who we are. It isn't about being happy all the time, everyone has hard days, good self-esteem is more about being able to move through those days with some kindness toward yourself. Feeling loved and valued by a parent or trusted adult is one of the biggest things that builds it, so the everyday stuff, hugs, "I love you," genuinely asking how they are, matters more than it might seem.

What can lower it

Self-esteem naturally rises and falls, especially around big changes like a new school. Longer-term low self-esteem tends to come from repeated negative messages, being labelled or criticised, pressure to always achieve, or comparison to unrealistic images of what someone "should" look like. It can also follow stressful experiences like bullying, discrimination, or abuse, or from feeling made to feel "different" because of a disability, neurodivergent condition, race, or identity such as being LGBTQIA+.

Signs to look out for

  • Talking negatively about themselves or their appearance
  • Comparing themselves unfavourably to others
  • Withdrawing from social situations or activities
  • Avoiding new things, or struggling to move on from mistakes
  • Seeming low in mood or less interested in things they used to enjoy
  • Turning to coping mechanisms like self-harm

Talking to them about it

Check in during a shared activity rather than a formal sit-down, and lead with an "I" statement, "I've noticed you've been staying in your room a lot, is everything okay?" Focus on listening and validating rather than fixing straightaway, "that sounds really hard" does more than jumping to solutions. If bullying, abuse, or discrimination is behind it, remember their self-esteem can't really recover while it's still happening, so addressing the root cause matters as much as the conversation itself.

Helping them build it back up

Praise specific things you notice ("you were really kind to your friend this week") rather than general praise like "good job," specific praise helps them recognise their own strengths. Show you value who they are, not just what they achieve, and let them know it's okay to find things hard sometimes. When they criticise themselves, gently offer a different, specific example of how you see them. Encourage activities that build a sense of belonging, a club, a hobby, a community project, and where relevant, connecting with others who share their identity or experience can help them feel less alone in it. And it helps to model this yourself: showing your own kind, accepting attitude toward yourself teaches them that it's allowed.

Getting further support

Their school can often help through mentoring, clubs, or peer support groups. A counsellor or therapist can work with them directly to explore where the feelings have come from. If low self-esteem is leading to persistent low mood, anxiety, or self-harm, speak to your GP.

Where to get help: YoungMinds' Parents Helpline (0808 802 5544) has advice on supporting self-esteem. Childline (0800 1111) offers confidential support directly to your child. Where identity is a factor, Black Minds Matter, Muslim Youth Helpline, and the National Autistic Society all offer more specific, tailored support.

What to Do If Your Child Is Self-Harming

Topic: Self-Harm

Understanding why a young person might self-harm, how to respond, and where to get professional support.

Self-harm is when someone hurts themselves on purpose as a way of coping with distressing or overwhelming feelings. It isn't usually about seeking attention, and it doesn't necessarily mean someone wants to end their life, though it should always be taken seriously. For many young people, it can feel like the only way to manage feelings that seem too big to hold, or a way to feel something when they've been feeling numb.

Signs to look out for

  • Unexplained injuries, or old injuries that don't seem to heal
  • Covering up more than usual, even in warm weather, or avoiding changing in front of others or activities like swimming
  • Seeming low or withdrawn, pulling away from friends and family
  • Talking about feeling like a failure, hopeless, or blaming themselves
  • Sudden outbursts of anger or irritability

None of these prove anything on their own, but together, or alongside a gut feeling that something's wrong, they're worth gently checking in about.

Why a young person might self-harm

It's often not really about the injury itself, it's a way of managing very strong feelings: relieving unbearable tension, feeling a sense of control when everything else feels out of control, or breaking through numbness. It tends to bring only temporary relief, so as feelings build up again, the urge often returns too, which can become a difficult cycle, often alongside shame or fear about what's happening.

Talking to them about it

Try opening the conversation somewhere neutral, and ideally while doing something else together, walking or driving takes some of the pressure off. Let them lead on when and how much they share, and focus on listening rather than reacting or trying to fix things immediately. It's natural to have a lot of questions, but try not to bombard them, and know that pressuring them to stop, or minimising it as "just a phase," tends to push young people away rather than help.

Supporting them day to day

Helping them notice what builds up before the urge to self-harm, what they're feeling, what's going on around them, can help both of you get ahead of it over time, a shared "mood word" each day or a journal can help without adding pressure. It also helps to think together, gently, about anything in the environment that feels risky, and to keep spending ordinary, enjoyable time together alongside the harder conversations. Try to stay involved without it feeling like surveillance, feeling constantly watched can add to a young person's guilt rather than ease it.

Keeping them safe

Any injury that needs medical attention should be seen by a GP or, if serious, at A&E. Self-harm doesn't necessarily mean someone is suicidal, but people who've self-harmed are statistically more likely to also experience suicidal thoughts, so it's worth staying alert to that too. If your child's life feels at risk, or they don't feel safe, call 999 or go straight to A&E.

Finding professional help

Your GP is a good first step and can refer to CAMHS or a mental health specialist if needed, you can also speak to them yourself even if your child doesn't want to. School can be a valuable source of support too, alongside counselling or therapy for your child specifically. It's normal for this to take time, and for your child to need space to build trust with a new professional.

Where to get help: If your child's life is at risk, call 999 or go to A&E immediately. YoungMinds' Parents Helpline (0808 802 5544) and Childline (0800 1111) can guide you on next steps. Samaritans (116 123, free, 24/7) is a listening service open to anyone, including parents finding this difficult. The Calm Harm app, built by clinicians specifically for this, offers young people a private way to manage urges between conversations.

Growing Up Online

Topic: Social Media

The real benefits and risks of being online, how to talk about it, and handling the harder conversations around safety.

Being online is a normal part of most young people's lives, and it genuinely offers real things: connection, creativity, learning, community, and a place to explore who they are. It also comes with real risks: comparison, cyberbullying, exposure to harmful content, and pressure to stay constantly connected. The goal isn't to eliminate one side or the other, it's helping your child navigate both.

How to talk about it

You don't need to be an expert in the platforms they use, what matters is that they feel able to come to you when something doesn't feel right. Help them think critically about what they see, how images are filtered or curated, how algorithms are designed to keep them scrolling, and encourage them to follow accounts that make them feel good rather than worse. Validate their feelings if something online has upset them, "it's just the internet" dismisses something that felt very real to them. Keep checking in during ordinary moments, not just when there's a problem, and try not to panic if something goes wrong, a calm response keeps the door open for next time.

Setting boundaries

Boundaries should shift as your child grows, more built-in for younger children, more collaborative as they get older. Sitting down together to agree ground rules, screen-free times, keeping devices out of bedrooms at night, works better than rules imposed unilaterally. Parental controls help for younger children, but as they get older, building their own judgement matters just as much as any technical setting.

Key risks to know about

  • Inappropriate content: young people can stumble on distressing material even with safety tools in place. Focus conversations on why something is harmful, not shame, and make clear they can always come to you about anything they've seen.
  • Overuse: look out for trouble sleeping, low mood when offline, or withdrawing from in-person activities. "Balance" tends to land better as a framing than "addiction."
  • Comparison and self-esteem: encourage unfollowing accounts that make them feel worse, and talk openly about how online life is always a curated highlight reel.
  • Peer pressure: to post, respond instantly, or join trends. Reassure them it's okay to step back from anything that feels overwhelming or inauthentic.
  • Cyberbullying: can follow a young person into their own bedroom, unlike in-person bullying. Take it seriously, help them block, report, and save evidence if needed.
  • Contact from strangers: including adults misrepresenting their age. Go through privacy and blocking features together, and make clear it's never their fault if someone behaves inappropriately toward them online.

Sexting and sexual content

This is an uncomfortable conversation for most parents, but an important one. Be calm and open rather than reactive, and make clear that no one has the right to ask them for an intimate photo or video, whatever their relationship to that person, and they never have to send anything that makes them uncomfortable. If your child tells you something has already happened, or you discover it yourself, stay calm, they're likely already frightened or ashamed. Let them know you're glad they told you, and that the law exists to protect them, not punish them, for seeking help. Organisations like CEOP, NSPCC, and Childline can talk you through next steps.

Where to get help: NSPCC (0808 800 5000) can advise on any online safety concern for a child. CEOP handles concerns about grooming or exploitation specifically. Childline (0800 1111) offers confidential support directly to your child. Internet Matters and the UK Safer Internet Centre offer practical, up-to-date guidance for parents.

If You're Worried About Suicidal Thoughts

Topic: Suicidal Thoughts

How to recognise the signs, talk about it directly and safely, and get the right help urgently.

Many young people have thoughts about suicide at some point, and having these thoughts doesn't automatically mean someone will act on them. But every suicidal feeling deserves to be taken seriously, and you're right to act on your concern promptly rather than wait and see.

Signs to look out for

  • Talking about feeling hopeless, worthless, or like a burden to others
  • Saying things like "I can't go on" or "people would be better off without me"
  • Withdrawing from friends, family, or things they used to enjoy
  • Giving away possessions, or talking, writing, or drawing a lot about death
  • Seeming unusually agitated, or acting out of character
  • Using drugs or alcohol to cope, or self-harming

Not every young person shows these signs clearly, some will say directly what they're feeling, others won't. If you're worried, trust that instinct enough to check in, even without a checklist of proof.

Talking about it directly

It's completely natural to worry about saying the wrong thing, but talking about suicidal thoughts does not increase risk or "put the idea in their head," the evidence is clear on this. You can start gently, "I've noticed you seem really low lately, I'm wondering what's going on," but at some point it matters to ask a direct, clear question using words like "suicide" or "ending your life," rather than vaguer phrases. Direct language tells them you're genuinely ready to hear the answer, whatever it is.

If they tell you they're having these thoughts

Stay as calm as you can, and thank them for telling you, it likely took real courage. Focus on listening rather than fixing anything straightaway, and try not to downplay what they're feeling. Gently try to understand how far the thoughts have gone, whether it feels like a passing thought or something they've started planning in more detail, since that difference matters for how urgently to act. Reassure them you're glad they told you and that you'll get through this together, then get professional advice about next steps, even if you don't think they're at immediate risk.

If they're not ready to talk

Try texting, writing a letter, or talking during a shared activity like a walk, sometimes that feels easier than a direct conversation. Encouraging them to speak to another trusted adult, or to a helpline, can help too. You can't force the conversation, but you can still act on your concern by seeking professional help yourself.

Getting urgent help

If your child has made a suicide attempt or seriously hurt themselves, call 999 immediately. If you believe they're at risk of doing so, or aren't safe right now, call 999 or take them straight to A&E, a mental health emergency is treated exactly as seriously as a physical one, and you won't be wasting anyone's time. If it isn't immediately life-threatening but you need urgent advice, call NHS 111 (24/7) and select the mental health option. If it's not urgent but you're still concerned, book an on-the-day GP appointment, they can assess, advise, and refer for specialist support.

Looking after yourself

Supporting a child through this is genuinely exhausting, and it's common to feel like you're living on high alert. You can't supervise them every hour of every day, and needing more support than you can provide at home doesn't mean you've failed, sometimes it means the right next step is more structured care. Talk to someone you trust, and don't carry this entirely on your own.

Where to get help: In an emergency, call 999 or go to A&E. NHS 111 (24/7) can advise if it's urgent but not life-threatening. Papyrus HOPELINE247 (0800 068 4141) specialises in supporting under-35s having suicidal thoughts, and anyone worried about a young person. Shout (text 85258) and Childline (0800 1111) offer free, confidential 24/7 support for your child. Samaritans (116 123, free, 24/7) is there for you too, not just your child.

Supporting a Trans or Non-Binary Child

Topic: Trans & Non-Binary Support

Supporting a trans or non-binary young person's mental health and wellbeing.

Trans and non-binary young people can face additional mental health pressures, from questioning their identity to how they're treated by others, and having a supportive adult makes a significant difference.

Listen without judgement, use the name and pronouns they ask you to, and connect with specialist support if they, or you, want it.

Where to get help: Mermaids offers support for trans, non-binary, and gender-diverse young people and their families.

Helping Your Child Through Change

Topic: Transitions & Change

Helping your child through big changes, like moving school, home, or family structure.

Transitions, new schools, house moves, new siblings, can unsettle even resilient young people, bringing up anxiety or behaviour changes even when the change itself is positive.

Talk about what's coming in advance where possible, keep some things consistent during the change, and give extra patience and reassurance while they adjust.

Where to get help: Your child's school pastoral team can support with transitions like starting a new school.

How Trauma Lingers, and How to Help

Topic: Trauma

What trauma is, how it can show up in a young person, and how to support their recovery.

A traumatic experience usually involves a threat to someone's sense of physical or emotional safety, often alongside feeling trapped, powerless, or unsupported. It can be a single event or something that happens repeatedly over months or years, and it can be something a young person went through directly or witnessed happening to someone else. Whether something is traumatic really depends on how it affects that individual, there's no fixed list, it can include things like a violent event, discrimination, abuse, bullying, bereavement, serious illness, or exposure to frightening content online. Trauma itself isn't a mental health condition, but it can contribute to things like anxiety, depression, OCD, eating disorders, or self-harm.

Signs to look out for

  • Flashbacks or intrusive thoughts about what happened
  • Difficulty managing emotions, or feeling overwhelmed easily
  • Acting out, angry outbursts, or withdrawing from friends, family, or usual activities
  • Avoiding anything that reminds them of the experience
  • Seeming constantly on alert, or the opposite, zoned-out and disconnected
  • Trouble concentrating, sleeping, or nightmares
  • Self-blame, guilt, or a drop in self-esteem

Every young person's response is different, some show it straightaway, others only later, and the link back to what happened isn't always obvious, sometimes not even to them.

PTSD and complex PTSD

Some young people go on to experience PTSD, where these symptoms are more intense and start significantly affecting daily life. Complex PTSD is sometimes diagnosed when trauma happened repeatedly over an extended period, such as ongoing childhood abuse or neglect. Both usually need professional support to improve, so it's worth speaking to your GP about an assessment if you're concerned.

Supporting their recovery

Healing from trauma takes time, it's not usually a straight line, there can be better stretches followed by harder ones. Give them space to talk when they're ready, and reflect back their feelings without minimising them, it's fine to use strong words like "terrified" or "furious" if that's genuinely what they're describing. Predictability and routine help rebuild a sense of safety, alongside plenty of reassurance that you're there and that you'll find the right support together. If whatever caused the trauma is still ongoing, bullying or an unsafe situation at home, for instance, that needs to be addressed directly, recovery can't really begin while it's still happening. Alongside the harder conversations, keep making space for ordinary, enjoyable time together too.

Finding professional support

Look for a counsellor or therapist with specific training in trauma, a trauma-informed approach makes a real difference. Your GP can refer to CAMHS for a specialist assessment and treatments such as trauma-focused CBT or EMDR. Their school can often provide additional pastoral support alongside any clinical treatment, and it's worth being open with them about what your child needs, with your child's input on what's shared and with whom.

Where to get help: Speak to your GP about assessment and referral to CAMHS. Childline (0800 1111) offers confidential support directly to your child. CALM (0800 58 58 58, 5pm–midnight) supports anyone 16+ who needs to talk. Where race or identity is a factor, Black Minds Matter and Muslim Youth Helpline offer more specific, tailored support.

Starting University: The Mental Load

Topic: University & Mental Health

Supporting your child's mental health as they head off to university.

The transition to university brings independence, new pressures, and reduced day-to-day support from home, all at once, which can be a significant adjustment even for young people who were doing well before.

Stay in regular, low-pressure contact, make sure they know how to register with a GP and access university wellbeing services, and let them know it's okay to ask for help.

Where to get help: Most universities have a student wellbeing or counselling service, register with these early rather than waiting for a crisis.

Stuck on a Waiting List? Here's What Helps

Topic: Waiting Lists

How to support your child while waiting for mental health services.

Waiting times for CAMHS and other mental health services can be long, and that gap can feel frightening when your child needs support now.

Ask what support is available while you wait, many services offer interim contact, keep a record of any changes so you can update the service, and don't hesitate to flag if things get worse while waiting.

Where to get help: Ask your GP or CAMHS about interim support, and contact YoungMinds' Parents Helpline for advice on navigating waits.

Unveiled ("we", "us", "our") is a nonprofit organisation registered in England and Wales (Charity No: [INSERT]). Our registered address is [INSERT ADDRESS]. This Privacy Policy explains what personal data we collect, why we collect it, how we use it, and your rights in relation to it. What Data We Collect

We may collect: your name, email address, phone number, and address when you register for our programme or contact us; the name, date of birth, and health information of young people registered with our programme (with parental consent); information you provide through our registration, consent, and referral forms; technical data such as your IP address and browser type when you visit our website (via cookies, see our Cookie Policy); and feedback and survey responses you submit.

Why We Collect It

We collect personal data to deliver our programme and support services, communicate with participants and their families, keep participants safe (including fulfilling our safeguarding obligations), measure the impact of our work and report to funders (using anonymised data only), improve our website and services, and comply with legal obligations.

How Long We Keep Your Data

We retain personal data only as long as necessary. Safeguarding records are kept for a minimum of 7 years, or until a young person reaches age 25 (whichever is longer). Programme registration data is kept for the duration of the programme plus 2 years. Website enquiry data is deleted after 12 months if no further contact is made.

Your Rights

Under UK GDPR, you have the right to:

  • Access — request a copy of the personal data we hold about you.
  • Rectification — ask us to correct data that is inaccurate or incomplete.
  • Erasure — ask us to delete your data ("the right to be forgotten"), where there's no legal reason for us to keep it.
  • Restrict processing — ask us to limit how we use your data while a concern is looked into.
  • Data portability — request your data in a format you can transfer to another organisation.
  • Object — object to us processing your data for certain purposes, including direct marketing.
  • Withdraw consent — where we rely on your consent to process your data, withdraw that consent at any time.
  • Complain — lodge a complaint with the Information Commissioner's Office (ICO) at ico.org.uk if you're unhappy with how we've handled your data.

To exercise any of these rights, contact our Data Controller using the details below. We will respond within one calendar month, as required by law.
